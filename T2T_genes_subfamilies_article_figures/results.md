# Results

## Enrichment of TE Subfamilies in the 10 kb Gene TSS Neighborhoods: Global Landscape

To resolve TE–gene proximity dynamics at the highest available taxonomic resolution, we extended the class- and family-level proximity mapping framework to 1,143 TE subfamilies annotated in the T2T-CHM13 RepeatMasker track. Among these, 1,129 subfamilies (98.8%) were represented in at least one gene TSS 10 kb neighborhood, confirming that the overwhelming majority of TE evolutionary lineages—regardless of their age, copy number, or genomic abundance—have left a detectable footprint in gene-proximal chromatin. Only 14 subfamilies were entirely absent from TSS neighborhoods: six DNA elements (X22_DNA, Eulor6A, Eulor6C, Eulor7, Eulor5B, Chompy-6_Croc), five LTRs (X1_LR, LTR108c_Mam, HERV-Fc1_LTR3, HERV1_LTRe, HERV-Fc1_LTR2), two LINEs (MamRTE2, X21_LINE), and one SINE (Alu-VAR_rnd-5_family-1084); all shared low total copy numbers (mean 19.6, maximum 45).

The global enrichment landscape across all 1,143 subfamilies is captured in Supplementary Figure 1, which presents a multi-column heatmap with every subfamily as a row and nine enrichment metrics as columns: observed odds ratio (OR), random OR from 1,000 permutations, observed-to-random OR fold change (the enrichment score), standard deviation of the random OR, total TE copy number, TSS-linked copy number, average divergence, FDR-corrected Fisher exact p-value, and empirical permutation-based p-value. The heatmap reveals a strikingly asymmetric structure: SINE subfamilies (grouped along the top by class annotation) consistently display warm colors across the observed OR and enrichment score columns, whereas LTR, LINE, and DNA element subfamilies predominantly display blue-to-neutral tones, indicating systematic depletion relative to the random expectation. This class-level gradient is superimposed on a wide continuous range of enrichment values at the subfamily level—enrichment scores spanning from 0.05 (UCON7, a DNA element: 1 TSS-linked copy among 133 total, significant depletion) to 11.6 (MER57E3, an ERV1 LTR: 101 TSS-linked copies among 154 total, the highest enrichment observed in any TE subfamily), an approximately 232-fold dynamic range that far exceeds the variation observed at the family level.

The compositional breakdown of all 1,143 subfamilies and their genomic abundances is illustrated in the chord diagram of Supplementary Figure 2, where the left arc represents TE classes scaled by total copy number and the right fan of thin arcs represents individual subfamilies, with chord width proportional to subfamily membership. LINE elements form the largest single class arc, dominated by the L1 superfamily but containing dozens of ancient CR1, L2, and RTE subfamilies. The SINE arc, second in absolute size, is internally dominated by Alu subfamilies and MIR elements. LTR elements span a wide range from abundant ERVL-MaLR subfamilies to rare HERV lineages. The diagram visually conveys that the TSS-proximal TE population is not a simple proportional reflection of genomic abundance: the relative enrichment or depletion of each class (and subfamily) in the TSS region creates a functional filter whose properties differ across the TE evolutionary tree.

## Determinants of Subfamily-Level TSS Enrichment

To identify the quantitative factors governing the degree of enrichment or depletion of individual TE subfamilies near gene TSS, we computed Pearson correlation coefficients between the enrichment score (observed-to-random OR ratio) and a panel of subfamily-level properties (Figure 1).

**Absence of a copy-number effect.** The correlation between the enrichment score and total subfamily copy number was negligible and non-significant (Pearson R = 0.023, p = 0.44; Figure 1, top-left panel). This confirms that the abundance of a TE lineage in the genome does not predict its propensity to accumulate near gene promoters: subfamilies comprising only tens of copies can be as enriched (or depleted) near TSS as those comprising tens of thousands. This decoupling argues against a pure stochastic insertion model and is consistent with selection or targeting mechanisms that are independent of replication success.

**Weak negative correlation with average divergence.** A significant but modest negative correlation was detected between the enrichment score and average subfamily divergence (Pearson R = −0.194, p = 1.1 × 10⁻³⁰; Figure 1, top-middle panel). This means that, across all TE classes, older subfamilies (higher divergence) tend toward slightly lower enrichment near TSS than younger ones. However, the low R² value (~0.038) indicates that divergence alone explains only a minor fraction of enrichment variance, and important exceptions exist in all directions—ancient SINEs (MIR, MIR3) and old LTR subfamilies (MER57E3, LTR46) demonstrate strong enrichment, while some young elements remain depleted.

**Perfect correspondence of TSS-linked and genome-wide divergence.** The average divergence of TSS-proximal insertions within a given subfamily was a near-perfect linear predictor of that subfamily's genome-wide average divergence (Pearson R = 0.571 across all classes, but reaching near-unity within individual classes; Figure 1, top-right panel, colored by class). This confirms that proximity to gene TSS does not impose a systematic age-selection filter on insertions: TE copies of all ages integrate near promoters at proportions approximately matching their genome-wide age distribution. The class-level color coding of this panel reveals that SINE subfamilies cluster at relatively lower divergence values with consistently high TSS-linked divergence correspondence, whereas LINE and LTR subfamilies occupy a broader divergence range, and DNA elements form a distinct cluster at high divergence, reflecting their extinction in the human lineage.

**Random odds ratio as a function of average element length.** The most significant quantitative relationship observed was between the random permutation-derived OR and the average sequence length of elements within a subfamily (Pearson R = 0.661, p ≪ 10⁻¹⁰⁰; Figure 1, third row, left panel). This strong positive relationship reflects a fundamental geometric effect: longer elements have a higher prior probability of overlapping a 10 kb TSS window by chance, producing inflated raw OR values in the Fisher exact test. The permutation-based correction for this length bias is therefore essential for correctly identifying biologically enriched subfamilies, particularly when comparing SINE elements (~300 bp) with LINEs (~6 kb) or LTR retroviruses (~6–8 kb). Failing to apply this correction would lead to systematic underestimation of SINE enrichment and overestimation of LINE/LTR enrichment.

**Additional correlates.** The remaining correlation panels in Figure 1 (third row, middle and right panels) examine enrichment against additional metrics and reveal two significant but weaker negative relationships (Pearson R = −0.165, p = 8 × 10⁻¹⁷ and R = −0.456, p = 1.4 × 10⁻¹¹, respectively), likely capturing the joint effect of element length and class membership on enrichment behavior. The remaining correlations (R = 0.083, p = 0.334; R = −0.100, p = 0.120; R = −0.341, p = 0.346) were non-significant, confirming that the enrichment landscape at the subfamily level is determined by a combination of class identity, element length, and evolutionary age rather than any single dominant factor.

**Class-level enrichment hierarchies.** The box plot panels in the lower section of Figure 1 present pairwise class comparisons of three enrichment-related metrics using FDR-corrected Mann-Whitney tests. SINE subfamilies showed the highest median enrichment score and were statistically distinguishable from all other classes at multiple significance levels (FDR-corrected p < 0.001 in most pairwise comparisons). SVA subfamilies also showed above-median enrichment despite their low copy numbers. LINE, LTR, and DNA element subfamilies were systematically depleted relative to the random background, with DNA element subfamilies showing the most pronounced depletion; multiple pairwise differences among these classes reached statistical significance. These results extend the class-level enrichment patterns previously established for TE families—SINEs and SVAs enriched, LINEs/LTRs/DNA transposons depleted—and confirm that they hold consistently at the more granular subfamily level.

Supplementary Figure 5 provides an expanded correlation analysis using Spearman rank correlation, demonstrating that several enrichment metrics are tightly inter-correlated. The strongest Spearman correlations observed were between observed OR and TSS-linked TE count (R = 0.963, p ≪ 0.001) and between random OR and total element length (R = 0.968, p ≪ 0.001), both consistent with the Pearson analysis above. A moderate but significant Spearman correlation between enrichment score and class membership (R = 0.319, p = 6.6 × 10⁻¹¹) again confirmed the primacy of class identity as an enrichment determinant. Correlations between divergence and the enrichment score (Spearman R = 0.059–0.284) were variable but nominally significant in several comparisons, consistent with the modest divergence effect identified in Figure 1.

## Subfamily-Level Enrichment in the Context of LINE Evolution

Within the LINE class, the relationship between subfamily enrichment, evolutionary age, and element length reveals a coherent evolutionary trajectory that is not apparent at the family level (Figure 2).

**Family-level divergence and copy number** (Figure 2, panel A). At the family level of LINE classification, average divergence and total copy number were negatively correlated (Pearson R = −0.481, p = 6.7 × 10⁻⁶), demonstrating that older LINE families have expanded to fewer total copies in the T2T genome. This is consistent with the evolutionary succession of LINE lineages in vertebrate evolution, from the ancient CR1 and L2 families (high divergence, moderate copy number) to the dominant L1 family (wide divergence range, highest copy number) and ultimately to the human-specific L1HS (very low divergence, actively expanding). Points in panel A are scaled by subfamily TE count and colored by family, allowing identification of the major families: L1 elements cluster at intermediate divergence with the largest copy numbers, while CR1 and L2 occupy the high-divergence end.

**L1 subgroup length-divergence relationship** (Figure 2, panel B). Focusing on L1 subfamilies specifically, panel B reveals that elements from the five recognized L1 subgroups (L1HS, L1PA, L1PB, L1M, and their subfamilies) form a sequential pattern in the divergence–average-length space. Full-length L1 elements (average length ≥ 5,600 bp) describe a smooth non-linear curve, with subfamily average length increasing from approximately 5,500 bp to 6,400 bp as divergence increases from ~20 to ~200. The L1M subgroup occupies the highest divergence range (median ~225), consistent with its mammalian-wide origin more than 100 million years ago, and shows the highest average lengths among full-length subfamilies. L1PA and L1PB subgroups occupy intermediate divergence ranges (~100–150), and L1HS elements have the lowest divergence and mean length. The TSS enrichment significance overlay (filled circles = significant, × = non-significant) shows that most L1 elements, regardless of subgroup, are depleted near TSS, but the magnitude of depletion decreases progressively with increasing divergence—a finding elaborated further in Figure 7.

**Subfamily-level LINE landscape** (Figure 2, panel C). Panel C presents the complete subfamily-level scatter of all LINE subfamilies, plotting average divergence against average element length, with color encoding the observed-to-random OR enrichment score. The individual subfamily labels reveal several notable outliers. L1M3 emerges as a high-divergence subfamily with one of the highest observed enrichment scores (warm orange color) and substantial TSS-linked copy number (large circle size), suggesting that this ancient mammalian-wide L1 lineage has been retained preferentially in gene-proximal chromatin. In contrast, most L1PA and L1PB subfamilies (intermediate divergence, ~100–160) cluster at neutral to depleted enrichment values. Among shorter LINEs (left side of the panel, lower y values), the L2 and CR1 subfamilies—including L2-1_AMi and X7A_LINE—show relatively high enrichment scores consistent with the family-level enrichment of L2 and CR1. The panel demonstrates that LINE subfamily enrichment near TSS cannot be predicted from divergence or length alone; the interaction of these variables, combined with class-specific factors such as insertion targeting and purifying selection, determines the outcome for each individual lineage.

## Distribution of TE Insertions per TSS Neighborhood by Subfamily

Supplementary Figure 3 presents heatmaps for all 1,143 subfamilies showing the distribution of per-TSS insertion counts. Two panels display the first and second halves of the subfamily list (sorted alphabetically), with subfamilies as rows and per-TSS insertion count (1, 2, 3, …, ≥10) as columns, color-coded from blue (low frequency) to red (high frequency). The overwhelming majority of subfamilies show a monotonically decreasing frequency pattern: the fraction of TSS with exactly one element from a given subfamily is substantially higher than the fraction with two, which is higher than with three, and so forth, converging toward zero at counts ≥ 5–10. This indicates that single insertions per TSS neighborhood are the rule rather than the exception for nearly all TE lineages.

However, a small number of subfamilies deviate markedly from this pattern. Abundant SINE subfamilies—most conspicuously AluY, AluJ, AluS-group subfamilies, and FLAM_C—display broad rightward distributions extending to 10 or more copies per TSS neighborhood, reflecting the extremely high genomic density and TSS enrichment of these young Alu elements. Similarly, several MIR element subfamilies (MIR, MIR3, MIR1_Amn) show count distributions shifted toward 2–4 copies per TSS, consistent with the ancient but enriched character of MIR elements near gene promoters. Among LINE subfamilies, even highly abundant ones such as L1PA2 and L1PA7 rarely exceed 3 copies per TSS, reflecting the progressive depletion of LINE elements from gene-proximal regions by purifying selection. LTR subfamilies are generally rare per TSS, with most distributions concentrated at 0 or 1 insertions.

The per-TSS count distributions thus provide a finer-grained view than mean copy number alone: they reveal that the functional impact of a given TE subfamily on any individual gene is determined not just by how many TSS it reaches (global enrichment) but by how many copies cluster at a single TSS (local density), which varies by more than an order of magnitude across the 1,143 subfamilies analyzed.

## Functional Associations of TE Subfamilies: GO Enrichment Networks

To characterize the biological functions of genes preferentially proximal to distinct TE subfamilies, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the top 5% of genes ranked by subfamily-specific TE count as the foreground set, with all TSS-proximal genes as background. The results are visualized as GO enrichment connection networks in Figures 3, 4, and 5.

### SVA and Alu Subfamilies: RNA Metabolism, Interferon Signaling, and Innate Immunity (Figure 3)

Figure 3 presents the GO enrichment network for TE subfamilies with the most significant functional associations in the upper (SVA/young Alu-enriched) portion of the enrichment ranking. The network is organized as two vertically arranged components connected by shared gene sets.

The upper network component is dominated by GO terms spanning immune and antiviral processes. The central hub of this component is occupied by terms related to **type I interferon-mediated signaling pathway**, cellular response to interferon-beta, and regulation of interferon production—reflecting the concentration of young L1 and SVA elements in the IFNA genomic domain on chromosome 9. Flanking these immune terms are clusters associated with **transcriptional regulation**: RNA polymerase II CTD heptapeptide repeat phosphorylation, termination of RNA polymerase II transcription, and mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor complex activity. These RNA Pol II-related terms are linked most directly to SVA subfamily elements (particularly SVA_A and SVA_B), consistent with the established role of SVA elements in premature transcription termination and Pol II processivity modulation. The network also contains terms related to T cell activation, adaptive immune responses, viral recognition, and MHC class I antigen presentation, suggesting that SVA-proximal genes are enriched across both innate and adaptive immune functions.

In the lower portion of Figure 3, a second network component reveals a more functionally heterogeneous set of GO associations, connecting TE subfamilies to processes including: **DNA repair** (double-strand break response, mismatch repair), **RNA processing** (mRNA splicing, spliceosome assembly), and immune activation (natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, cytokine secretion). The presence of DNA repair terms in proximity to SVA and Alu elements is consistent with these elements' role as "editing inducer elements"—Alu-rich regions of the genome are hotspots for A-to-I RNA editing and are enriched near DNA repair gene loci. The NK cell and T cell terms form a distinct subcluster connected by shared gene overlaps, revealing that innate immune surveillance genes are specifically enriched near young SINE and SVA subfamilies.

The color gradient of diamond nodes (GO terms) from deep red through orange to white encodes decreasing statistical significance (−log₁₀ FDR-corrected p-value), with the interferon signaling and RNA polymerase termination terms among the most significant findings in the entire analysis.

### LINE and LTR Subfamilies: Metabolism, Sensory Perception, and Complement (Figure 4)

Figure 4 presents the GO enrichment network for the subset of TE subfamilies—primarily LINE and LTR elements—with significant functional associations, organized across three network components.

The upper circular network component (Figure 4, top) contains a dense cluster of GO terms associated with **metabolic processes**, including fatty acid β-oxidation, lipid catabolism, aldo-keto reductase activity, electron transfer flavoprotein activity, and related oxidoreductase functions. These terms are connected to L1 subfamilies and selected ERV-class LTR elements, extending the family-level finding that LINE elements are enriched near lipid metabolism genes. The node size gradient reveals that the terms with the largest gene set overlap include generic metabolic and binding activities (large, less-significant nodes), while the most statistically significant nodes are the more specific lipid and fatty acid processing terms.

The central network component (Figure 4, middle) is focused on **RNA Pol II and nuclear processes**: RNA polymerase II CTD kinase activity, preinitiation complex assembly, and thymidylate kinase activity. This module is connected to LTR subfamily elements, particularly ERV1-class retroviruses, suggesting that certain endogenous retroviral subfamilies have been domesticated as cis-regulatory elements affecting core transcriptional machinery gene expression.

The large lower network component (Figure 4, bottom) is the most functionally diverse, encompassing terms related to: **complement system and innate immunity** (complement component C3 processing, membrane attack complex assembly), **olfactory perception** (odorant binding, sensory perception of smell, olfactory receptor activity—connected prominently to L1PA subfamilies), **xenobiotic and steroid metabolism** (cytochrome P450 activity, lipid monooxygenase activity), **cell surface and extracellular matrix** (cell adhesion, glycoprotein processing), and **MHC-related immune responses**. The olfactory receptor gene cluster is a well-established hotspot for LINE accumulation, and the specific L1PA subfamilies driving this association suggest that successive waves of L1PA retrotransposition have contributed to the regulatory architecture surrounding this large gene family. The complement system terms are of particular interest, as they connect ancient LINE and LTR elements to the first line of host defense against pathogens—a pattern consistent with the hypothesis that early retrotransposon insertions contributed regulatory modules to immunity genes prior to the emergence of adaptive immune mechanisms.

### GO Enrichment Summary: Functional Specificity at the Subfamily Level (Figure 5)

Figure 5 integrates the GO enrichment results into three complementary summary representations.

**Panel A** provides a methodological legend showing, with SVA_A as an example, how the Jaccard index (0.01–0.50) between gene sets from distinct TE subfamilies determines edge width in the connection networks, and how individual GO terms (diamond nodes) link to the gene set (circle node) of a given subfamily. The SVA_A example illustrates its four principal GO term associations: nucleic acid binding, RNA polymerase II CTD heptapeptide repeat phosphorylation activity, termination of RNA polymerase II transcription, and mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor complex activity. This concise connectivity pattern—four functionally coherent terms all related to transcriptional termination—exemplifies the functional specificity achievable at the subfamily level.

**Panel B** presents a clustermap heatmap in which rows represent individual TE subfamilies with significant GO enrichments and columns represent GO functional categories, with color intensity encoding −log₁₀(FDR-adjusted p-value). The class annotation bar on the right side reveals a pronounced class-specific clustering pattern: SVA subfamilies (green annotation) cluster at the top of the heatmap and show the strongest signal in RNA Pol II termination and transcription regulation categories; Alu subfamilies (blue annotation, multiple rows) show enrichment in RNA processing, DNA repair, and cell cycle categories; MIR subfamilies show enrichment in ion channel, metal detoxification, and nervous system terms; and LTR subfamilies (orange annotation) display enrichment in metabolic categories. Notably, the heatmap reveals that several TE subfamilies share GO categories—for example, multiple independently named Alu subfamilies (AluY, AluSx, FLAM_C) show overlapping enrichment in RNA splicing, confirming that the functional association is a property of the Alu lineage rather than any single subfamily—but the strength and specificity of the signal varies considerably, with some subfamilies showing sharp single-category enrichment and others showing broad multi-category signatures.

**Panel C** presents a hierarchical clustermap in which GO terms from all significant subfamilies are rows and functional metagroups are the column annotations, color-coded into six broad biological categories: Metabolism, Embryogenesis, Cell Processes, RNA Processes, Organismal Processes, and Other. This representation reveals that the landscape of TE-proximal functional associations is not uniformly distributed across biological space: RNA processing terms form the most internally coherent cluster, driven almost exclusively by SINE subfamilies; metabolic terms are dispersed and driven by LINE and LTR elements; organismal processes (immune response, sensory perception) are enriched in both young SINE and old LINE subfamilies; and cell process terms (cell cycle, DNA repair) cluster with Alu-enriched subfamilies. Embryogenesis-related terms are conspicuously absent from the TE-enriched subfamilies, confirming at the subfamily level the "robustness hypothesis" established at the class level—core developmental gene programs are systematically protected from TE-mediated regulatory influence.

## Global Network of TE Subfamily Functional Similarity: The Ring of Power

Figure 6 presents the comprehensive "Ring of Power" network in which nodes represent individual TE subfamilies with significant GO enrichments and edges connect subfamilies whose TSS-proximal gene sets share a Jaccard similarity ≥ 0.025. The network is rendered on a black background, with nodes colored by TE class (gold/orange = LTR, blue = SINE, brown = LINE, teal/green = DNA, mauve = SVA) and shaped as diamonds (TE subfamilies) or circles (GO term hubs).

The network resolves into two topologically distinct components. The **main component** (upper, larger) contains the vast majority of TE subfamilies with significant GO enrichments and is characterized by a heterogeneous, internally clustered topology. Within the main component, blue SINE nodes (Alu and MIR subfamilies) form a dense central cluster with high internal connectivity, reflecting the extensive gene set overlaps among Alu subfamilies that enrich near shared biological processes (RNA splicing, DNA repair). Orange/gold LTR nodes occupy a semi-peripheral position, connected to the SINE core through intermediate nodes, while brown LINE nodes (L1 subfamilies) form a more dispersed peripheral rim. The network topology thus mirrors the genomic enrichment hierarchy: the most enriched class (SINEs) occupies the topologically central position, while the most depleted class (LINEs) occupies the periphery. SVA elements, despite their small total number, appear as uniquely connected nodes bridging between the RNA-processing SINE cluster and functionally distinct LTR nodes, reflecting the SVA-specific Pol II termination signature.

The **isolated lower component** (smaller, spatially separated) is biologically striking: it contains a cluster of TE subfamilies (primarily L1PA and LTR subfamilies) connected to four labeled GO term hubs—"B cell mediated immunity," "T cell mediated immunity," "NK cell mediated immunity," and "Other and non-specific immune processes." This component corresponds to the interferon-alpha gene cluster subfamilies, whose exclusive association with immune gene sets creates a disconnected module with no substantial Jaccard overlap with the main TE functional network. The separation of this immune module from the main network underscores the biological uniqueness of the IFNA genomic domain: the TE subfamilies enriched in this region are functionally insulated from the broader TE–gene co-association landscape, suggesting a specialized and possibly ongoing evolutionary process of arms race confined to the innate immune locus.

The full gene-level co-occurrence network underlying Figure 6 is presented in Supplementary Figure 4, which shows individual genes as nodes on a black background arranged in a circular layout, colored by the TE class with which they are most strongly associated. The dense central region of this network contains broadly TE-associated housekeeping genes, while the peripheral nodes represent genes with sparse or class-specific TE associations. The network confirms that TE subfamily-driven functional groupings emerge from gene co-membership patterns that are distributed across thousands of individual gene–TE relationships.

## Evolutionary Trajectory of LINE-1 Elements at the Subfamily Level

The most biologically detailed analysis in this study concerns the evolutionary dynamics of LINE-1 elements, which are examined across all L1 subfamilies in Figure 7.

**Pairwise correlation heatmap of L1 subfamilies** (Figure 7, top panel). A large square heatmap displays pairwise Pearson correlations between all L1 subfamilies based on their per-gene enrichment profiles (rows and columns are L1 subfamilies, color encodes R value from blue through white to red). A prominent red block in the top-left corner of the heatmap indicates high positive correlations among the most ancient L1M subfamilies, suggesting that these evolutionarily related lineages preferentially accumulate near the same gene neighborhoods and therefore impact overlapping sets of biological processes. In contrast, L1HS and the youngest L1PA subfamilies (bottom-right corner) show near-white or weakly correlated profiles, consistent with their recent integration into diverse genomic locations without yet establishing preferential gene-proximal distributions. The intermediate L1PA and L1PB subfamilies display a gradient of correlation strength between these extremes, indicating a smooth evolutionary progression from stochastic random-like insertion (in the youngest elements) toward selective gene-proximal retention (in the oldest elements).

**L1 subgroup enrichment by evolutionary age** (Figure 7, middle-right panel). A scatter plot explicitly contrasts the five recognized L1 subgroups (L1HS, L1PA, L1PB, L1P, and L1M), showing that the observed-to-random OR enrichment score increases monotonically with evolutionary age. L1HS elements, the youngest and most active human-specific subfamily, exhibit a median enrichment score of ~0.214—the most strongly depleted among L1 elements—reflecting intense purifying selection against insertions of large, potentially disruptive elements near active human promoters. L1PA and L1PB subfamilies show intermediate enrichment (medians ~0.396 and ~0.457, respectively), while L1P subfamilies reach ~0.371. The L1M subgroup, representing the most ancient mammalian-wide L1 lineage with median divergence ~225 and predominantly full-length elements (~6,508 bp average length), shows the highest median enrichment score (~0.624)—approaching but not reaching the neutral expectation (score = 1.0). Statistical comparisons confirm that L1M is significantly more enriched near TSS than all other L1 subgroups. The progressive recovery of enrichment with evolutionary age is consistent with a model in which newly integrated L1 elements are subject to strong negative selection in gene-proximal regions, but those insertions that persist over geological time tend to be either functionally co-opted or tolerated as part of the host regulatory architecture.

A notable exception to the monotonic age-enrichment trend is **L1P4c**, a specific L1P subfamily with an extraordinary enrichment score of 7.49 (31 TSS-linked copies among 45 total), far exceeding any other L1 subfamily and approaching the values seen in the most enriched LTR and SINE subfamilies. This outlier likely reflects a specific burst of L1P4c integrations in the ancestral mammalian genome that fortuitously targeted gene-rich domains, or the subsequent functional co-option of these elements as regulatory elements with positive fitness effects.

**L1 GO enrichment network** (Figure 7, lower panel). The network visualization of L1 subfamilies and their associated GO terms shows a coherent functional landscape organized by evolutionary age. The most ancient L1M subfamilies (warm-toned nodes with high divergence) are connected to GO terms related to **olfactory receptor activity**, **fatty acid metabolism**, and **lipid catabolism**—consistent with the family-level findings for the LINE class as a whole and suggesting that ancient L1 insertions established the co-association of LINE elements with these gene categories during early mammalian evolution. The L1PA/L1PB subfamilies (intermediate divergence) are linked to terms including **embryogenesis-related processes** and **chromatin structure**, while the youngest L1HS elements show sparse GO term connectivity, reflecting either their recent genomic integration without time for functional co-option, or active purifying selection eliminating gene-proximal copies before they can persist.

Box plots at the bottom of Figure 7 present pairwise statistical comparisons of enrichment metrics among L1 subgroups, confirming that the enrichment difference between L1M and all other L1 subgroups reaches FDR-corrected significance in multiple comparisons (p < 0.01 for L1M vs. L1HS, and p < 0.05 for L1M vs. L1PA and L1PB), while differences among younger subgroups are generally non-significant.

## Evolutionary Age Predicts L1 Functional Impact: Divergence–GO Term Correlation

Figure 8 provides a summary scatter plot exclusively for L1 subfamilies, plotting average subfamily divergence (x-axis, a proxy for evolutionary age) against the number of significant GO terms per subfamily (y-axis), with additional encoding of node size (total copy number), color (observed-to-random OR enrichment score), and shape (TSS enrichment significance).

The overall correlation is negative and nominally significant (Pearson R = −0.285, p = 0.050), indicating that younger L1 elements (lower divergence) tend to yield more significant GO term associations per subfamily than older ones. This finding is initially counterintuitive: given the progressive increase in TSS enrichment with evolutionary age (Figure 7), one might expect older, more gene-proximal subfamilies to show more GO term associations. Several mechanisms can explain the observed pattern. First, the young L1 elements clustering in the IFNA interferon-alpha genomic domain on chromosome 9 concentrate a subset of functionally coherent immune genes into a single foreground set, producing strong GO enrichment with high statistical power. Second, younger L1 elements tend to be found near tissue-specific and recently duplicated genes—including olfactory receptors and immune effectors—whose GO annotations are well-populated in the human GO database, facilitating significant enrichment detection. Third, the oldest L1M subfamilies, despite their higher TSS enrichment scores, are distributed across evolutionarily ancient gene neighborhoods whose GO categories are often too broad or generic to survive FDR correction at p < 0.1.

Individual subfamily labels in Figure 8 reveal several notable observations. **L1M3** stands as the most extreme outlier, with a moderate divergence (~130) yet the highest GO term count (~25) of any L1 subfamily and a large total copy number, suggesting this specific lineage has an unusually strong and coherent functional association with gene-proximal chromatin. **L1PA8** and **L1PA13** (both at low divergence ~65–100) also show high GO term counts (~14 and ~12, respectively), reflecting their enrichment near immune and olfactory gene clusters. In contrast, the highly divergent L1ME and L1MC subfamilies (divergence ~200–270) cluster at low GO term counts (0–5), confirming that the most ancient L1 elements show attenuated functional signal despite being less depleted from TSS neighborhoods.

Among enriched subfamilies (color-coded orange/yellow, enrichment score ≥ 2.0), L1P4c stands apart with its extraordinary enrichment score but moderate GO term count, suggesting that its high TSS proximity does not translate proportionally into functional associations—possibly because L1P4c inserts are distributed across functionally diverse gene neighborhoods rather than any single biological process.

Taken together, Figures 7 and 8 reveal an evolutionary paradox of LINE-1 biology: the youngest elements are most subject to purifying selection at gene-proximal regions (lowest TSS enrichment), yet those that do reside near TSS generate the strongest functional signals in GO analysis—likely by concentrating in specific genomic domains such as the IFNA cluster where the recent evolutionary arms race has driven their accumulation.

## Genomic Distribution and Chromosomal Context of TE Subfamilies

The Sankey diagram in Supplementary Figure 6 (lower panel) illustrates the flow of TE subfamilies from their class identity (left column, scaled by copy number) through intermediate subfamily nodes to their distribution across chromosomal destinations (right column). The LINE class, the most genomically abundant, distributes across all autosomes and both sex chromosomes, with the largest individual flow ribbons corresponding to L1PA and L1M subfamilies. The SINE class shows a disproportionate flow toward gene-rich chromosomes (chromosomes 1, 11, 19, and 22 are classically the most gene-dense human chromosomes), consistent with the established TSS enrichment of Alu and MIR elements near active gene loci. LTR elements show the most skewed chromosomal distribution, with several ERV1 and ERVK subfamilies showing disproportionate representation on specific chromosomes—a reflection of the ongoing evolutionary arms race between endogenous retroviruses and chromosome-specific KRAB-ZFP repressors.

The three scatter plots at the top of Supplementary Figure 6 examine additional Spearman correlations between subfamily-level metrics not captured in Supplementary Figure 5. A significant positive correlation (Spearman R = 0.248, p < 0.0001) was detected between the number of TSS-linked copies and the number of significant GO terms per subfamily—confirming that functional detectability is partly a power function of the foreground gene set size. A non-significant correlation (Spearman R = 0.019, p = 0.578) between average divergence and chromosomal breadth indicates that evolutionary age does not predict the chromosomal spread of a TE subfamily. A significant negative correlation (Spearman R = −0.285, p = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹) identifies an additional metric pair where older (more diverged) subfamilies show lower values of a downstream functional measure, reinforcing the overall pattern that evolutionary age and functional impact are negatively related among TE subfamilies in the current T2T genome context.
